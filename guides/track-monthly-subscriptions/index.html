How to Track Monthly Subscriptions for Your Business

By Subastian · Updated November 3, 2026 · 4 min read

You can only cancel what you know you pay for. Business subscriptions spread across cards, PayPal, app stores and yearly plans, so the list in your head is almost never complete. Here is how to track monthly subscriptions in one place and keep the list up to date in about ten minutes a month.

The short version
  • Subscriptions hide on old cards, PayPal, app stores and yearly plans.
  • Record seven things for each: name, job, price, billing, next renewal, card, last used.
  • Spend 10 minutes a month adding new charges and checking the next 30 days of renewals.
  • Set reminders for yearly plans 30 days out, not on the day.

Where business subscriptions hide

Most people can name the big tools they pay for. The ones that slip through are spread across places you don't check every month:

  • Every card you've ever used for software, including old business cards and a personal card "just this once."
  • PayPal's automatic payments, which never show the tool's name on your card statement.
  • App Store and Google Play subscriptions on your phone.
  • Annual plans, which charge once and then disappear from view for a year.
  • Free trials that quietly turned into paid plans.
  • Tools a teammate or contractor signed up for on your card.

What to record for each subscription

Seven fields are enough to answer every question that matters later:

FieldWhy it matters
Tool nameSo you can find it on a statement
What it's forOne line on the job it does, which is how you spot two tools doing the same job
Price and billingMonthly or yearly. Divide yearly prices by 12 so totals compare fairly
Next renewal dateThe day you'd be charged next. The most important field
Card or account it bills toSo you know where to cancel or update it
Last time you used itAnything untouched for 30 days is a cancel candidate
Client or projectFor agencies: so a finished client's tools don't keep billing

Three ways to track them

1. A spreadsheet

Free and flexible. It works as long as you keep it updated, and that's the weak spot: a spreadsheet doesn't remind you of anything. If you go this way, put each renewal date in your calendar too.

2. A monthly statement review

Once a month, scan your card statements and PayPal for anything that repeats. It catches what the spreadsheet missed, but only after you've already paid for it once.

3. A subscription tracker

A tool built for the job keeps the list, warns you before renewals and free-trial end dates, and flags tools you've stopped using. Most of the big ones are built for companies with a finance team and an accounting system; see our comparison of subscription management tools for small businesses.

A 10-minute monthly routine

  1. Add anything new. Scan last month's card and PayPal charges for subscriptions that aren't on your list yet.
  2. Check the next 30 days of renewals. For each one, ask: did I use this in the last month?
  3. Check free trials. Cancel any you won't keep at least two days before they end.
  4. Update "last used." Anything idle for 30 days goes on a cancel-or-pause shortlist.
  5. Look for overlap. Two tools doing the same job? Keep the one you open most. Our duplicate subscriptions checklist helps.

Don't forget the yearly plans

Annual subscriptions are where the biggest surprise charges come from, because you see them once a year. Set reminders well before they renew, not on the day. We use a 30-7-3-1 reminder schedule: 30 days out to decide, 7 to cancel if needed, then 3 and 1 as a last check.
